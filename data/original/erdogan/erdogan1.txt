My dear brothers and sisters, I congratulate the administrators of SETA for organizing this symposium at a time when Türkiye stands on the eve of a historic decision to transition to the Presidential System.

Every country has a form of government unique to its own circumstances. When we look at our country, we see that over the past 200 years we have gone through many different experiences one after another.

My brothers and sisters, throughout this process our country has been worn down and diminished by wars and has paid very heavy prices. The Victory of Çanakkale represents the point at which we could no longer endure any further. Drawing inspiration and strength from Çanakkale, we fought our War of Independence.

While European countries chose to maintain monarchy alongside democracy, we sent the dynasty out of the country and proclaimed the Republic. First, we had a single-party system—please pay attention to this—and then a multi-party system of government, through which our country has reached the present day.

Since our transition to multi-party political life in 1950, we have always tried to keep our democracy standing under the shadow of military coups and tutelary administrations.

The most important consequence of these distortions has been the constant threat to an environment of stability and confidence. Political instability also brings economic and social problems with it.

Our search over the past 200 years, our experiences during the Republican era, and especially what we have gone through in recent years show us one reality: if we want to achieve our goals as a country and as a nation, we first need a strong and effective system of government in which it is completely clear who holds authority and responsibility.

In fact, the Presidential System that we are discussing today did not suddenly emerge in a day, a month, or a year. Behind it lies such a deep and thought-provoking historical background.

The system we are discussing is the most appropriate solution to the question of survival that Türkiye and the Turkish nation have faced for centuries. That is the issue.

When we bring together all these experiences, the events we have lived through, and our reading of history, our country's need for a new constitution and, along with it, a new system of government becomes as clear as day. My brothers and sisters, as you can see, the Presidential System we are discussing today has nothing to do with the regime, one-man rule, or personalization of power.

The issue is our effort, as a country and a nation, to establish a system of government that is much stronger, much more resilient, and much better suited to achieving our goals in light of the experiences we have had in the past.

The greatest safeguard of the Presidential System is that it is democratic in the true sense of the word. Under this system, no one who fails to win the support of more than half of our nation can have the opportunity to govern the country.

What we are engaged in here is a struggle over a system. This is a struggle over the system. What will happen after Erdoğan? Whatever the nation says will happen. Whatever God wills will happen.

More importantly, Türkiye has already taken its first steps toward this system and has successfully completed them. I am referring to the constitutional amendment of 2007.

Therefore, with the constitutional amendment, rather than personalizing the issue, we are placing governance within a system.

My brothers and sisters, without a soul, the body is merely a corpse. Likewise, if we do not explain to our nation the spirit, essence, and fundamental meaning of this issue, the provisions of the constitutional amendment alone will remain nothing more than dry legal expressions.

With this understanding, until the date of the referendum, until April 16, those of us who say “Yes” to the Presidential System of Government must travel across every corner of our country, use every means of communication, and explain the new system.

Why? Yes for one nation. Yes for one flag. Yes for one homeland. Yes for one state.

The essence of the Presidential System is that the authority to govern is given directly to the nation.

Because the President, who will perform the executive function, will be accountable to the nation from whom he derives his authority, he will have to act at every step knowing that the eyes of the public are upon him.

What did we say? Ours will be a Turkish-style Presidential System. We do not have to be exactly the same as others.

The President—that is, the head of the executive—will represent the executive branch. He will form his cabinet, appoint its members, and dismiss them when necessary. But to whom will he be accountable? He will be accountable to the nation. He will be accountable to the nation.

The executive branch, which currently has two heads—the President and the Prime Minister—will now be united under a single office. In this way, from the very beginning, our nation will know to whom it has given authority, who has assumed responsibility, and therefore whom it can hold accountable when necessary.

The legislative body and the members of parliament serving there will focus solely on their primary duties under the new system.

With the new system, which clearly separates the executive from the legislature, all members of parliament will be able to concentrate on parliamentary work.
